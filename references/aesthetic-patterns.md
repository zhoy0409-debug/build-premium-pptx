# Aesthetic pattern library

Use this reference to choose a visual grammar before selecting individual layouts. These patterns are original abstractions from strong academic, scientific, and analytical presentations; do not copy a source slide or redistribute source assets.

## The three-reading-speed rule

Every information-rich slide must work at three speeds:

1. **5 seconds — takeaway:** the title states the conclusion, active chapter, or decision.
2. **30 seconds — evidence:** 1–3 dominant charts, diagrams, images, or comparison blocks prove the title.
3. **Q&A — detail:** labels, source notes, methods, and secondary evidence remain readable without competing with the takeaway.

Density is earned by structure, not by smaller text. If the hierarchy fails at thumbnail size, reorganize the page. If projected text becomes too small, split the slide.

## Style archetypes

### Clear defense / 清晰答辩型

- White canvas, restrained institutional blue, thin dividers, one active chapter tab.
- Persistent top navigation is useful for long defenses; highlight only the current chapter.
- Prefer titled evidence zones, aligned scientific figures, and a short interpretation band.
- Best for thesis defense, opening report, progress review, and formal evaluation.

### Consulting nerdy / 咨询数据型

- Dense analytical canvas with a claim headline, spatial zones, direct annotations, and one decision implication.
- Combine a map, bubble plot, matrix, waterfall, or architecture diagram only when the relationship matters.
- Use one restrained accent family; let geometry and labels carry the complexity.
- Best for strategy, market analysis, operations, portfolio, risk, and data-heavy executive work.

### Institutional research / 院校品牌型

- Use a stable school or organization identity: logo safe area, consistent header, section rail, and footer.
- A vertical chapter rail works well for 30–50-page defenses because it gives orientation without repeating an agenda.
- Brand color may anchor headers and callouts, but evidence areas should remain neutral and readable.
- Best for university defenses, laboratory reports, grant reviews, and institutional briefings.

### Scientific editorial / 科学编辑型

- Pair a strong scientific hero illustration with an editorial title, restrained labels, and generous contrast.
- Body pages use pale scientific backgrounds, numbered sections, schematic diagrams, direct finding statements, and source lines.
- Repeat one visual motif across the deck—molecular structure, anatomical line art, network, or material texture—without using it as decoration on every slide.
- Best for paper interpretation, journal club, biomedical talk, and research communication.

### Research operating system / 科研系统型

- Use one deep navy canvas across a long technical lecture, with cyan or teal as the structural accent and a small set of semantic colors for tools, stages, or evidence classes.
- Treat the deck like a coherent interface: stable title position, thin frame lines, numbered modules, compact cards, and deliberate chapter pauses.
- Alternate dense system pages with sparse chapter dividers so a 30–70-page talk still has rhythm. A dark slide is not automatically dramatic; consistency and projection contrast make it usable for long-form teaching.
- Best for research methods, software workflows, AI-assisted research, technical training, and tool-heavy graduate lectures.

### Figure-first paper review / 图表优先文献汇报型

- Use a white evidence canvas, one restrained oxblood or institutional-red accent, a thin title rule, and a fixed slide-number chip.
- Let the original experiment figure dominate; added text should identify the question, method, result, and interpretation rather than repeat the paper caption.
- Repeat a scientific reasoning loop: new question → experiment or figure → result summary → next question. Use a clean mechanism diagram after each evidence cluster to reset understanding.
- Best for journal club, literature review, lab meeting, biomedical paper interpretation, and experimental result reporting.

## Reusable page grammars

| Pattern | Structure | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Chapter navigation | Persistent top tabs + one active tab + page title | The audience needs orientation across 5–7 chapters |
| Vertical section rail | Brand/logo zone + numbered chapter spine + content canvas | A long institutional defense needs a stable frame |
| Research objective | One-sentence objective + 2–3 evidence figures + synthesis band | Several mechanisms support one research aim |
| Evidence-to-decision | Visual evidence on the left + explicit conclusion/action rail on the right | The audience must know what the evidence changes |
| Method pipeline | Input/data collection → processing/analysis → validated result | A study or workflow must be reproducible |
| Innovation triptych | Three parallel claims, each with one proof figure | Technical, theoretical, and applied innovations must be compared |
| Anchored timeline | Step cards connected to a single axis + date labels | A project has 3–5 real phases with outputs |
| Workplan matrix | Years or quarters as columns + workstreams as rows | Multiple experiments or teams run in parallel |
| Regional analysis | Map as the primary canvas + bubbles/zones + forecast or recommendation | Geography affects priority or performance |
| Formula story | Input/condition + mechanism/evidence = result/decision | A compact causal or strategic argument is needed |
| Risk and response | Risk map on the left + matched mitigation table on the right | Every flagged risk needs an owner or action |
| Scientific cover | Editorial title + hero diagram + topic tags + citation/source | The topic itself has a recognisable scientific object |
| Research OS module grid | Stable dark frame + numbered cards + semantic accent per module | A long technical lecture compares tools, stages, prompts, or methods |
| Result reasoning loop | New question → evidence figure → direct finding → next question | A paper review must preserve the logic connecting multiple experiments |
| Figure summary reset | Simplified mechanism diagram + one-sentence conclusion band | Several dense result slides need a comprehension checkpoint |

## Narrative rhythm for research decks

### Long-form technical lecture

1. Open with the promise, not a generic topic label.
2. Show a compact map of the full system or workflow.
3. Use a sparse divider at every genuine conceptual shift.
4. Within each chapter, alternate principle cards, tool comparisons, workflow diagrams, evidence screenshots, and one synthesis page.
5. End each chapter with a reusable rule, checklist, or decision—not another recap paragraph.

### Journal club or literature review

1. Identify the paper, the biological or technical problem, and why the question matters.
2. Build only the background required to understand the first experiment.
3. For each result cluster, repeat: question → experimental evidence → result → short mechanism summary.
4. Separate what the authors showed from what the presenter infers.
5. Close with one integrated mechanism, limitations, and future work.

## Semantic color discipline

- Use one structural accent throughout the deck and at most four semantic accents inside analytical components.
- Reuse a semantic color for the same meaning across slides: input, method, warning, finding, or action.
- Do not color scientific figures merely to match the theme. Preserve data meaning first; use the presentation accent in labels, frames, and annotations around the figure.
- On dark canvases, verify body text, thin strokes, chart labels, and screenshots at projector contrast—not only on a laptop display.

## Evidence container rule

Every figure, chart, or screenshot should live in a deliberate container with at least two of these:

- a short label describing what it is;
- a direct annotation describing what matters;
- a source or method note;
- an interpretation or implication band.

Do not paste several raw figures and expect the audience to infer the argument. Crop redundant axes and whitespace only when scientific meaning remains intact.

## High-density guardrails

- Keep one dominant reading path: left-to-right, top-to-bottom, or center-out—not all three.
- Use 2–4 major zones on a normal slide. More zones require a strong grid and repeated component rules.
- Align figure edges, captions, and callout bands. Uneven near-alignments make dense pages look accidental.
- Use color for navigation, grouping, or emphasis; never color every block independently.
- Reserve saturated color for the active tab, key conclusion, or exception.
- Keep logos inside a fixed safe area and never let branding compete with the conclusion.
- Preserve projected readability. Dense academic figures may use smaller original labels, but added explanatory text must remain legible.

## Reject these false signals of professionalism

- many tiny figures with no synthesis;
- a full page of paragraphs disguised as cards;
- inconsistent scientific illustration styles;
- decorative arrows that do not encode direction or causality;
- active tabs, logos, or section labels that shift position between slides;
- multiple saturated colors without semantic meaning;
- screenshots used where an editable chart or clean redraw is possible.
